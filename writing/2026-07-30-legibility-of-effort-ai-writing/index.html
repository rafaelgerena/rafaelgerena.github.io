Legibility of Effort: Why Institutions Can No Longer Assume Human Judgment Behind Public Statements

By Rafael Gerena · July 30, 2026

Summary: A June 2026 essay by Nolen Royalty on eieio.games named a new problem: legibility of effort, the fading ability to tell whether a human put real work into something now that AI can produce plausible text instantly. Wired reported on July 29, 2026 that media institutions are responding directly. Pitchfork, under head of editorial content Mano Sundaresan, has banned AI from review copy and pushed writers toward first-person voice. Aeon editor Richard Fisher is urging writers to stay rough and idiosyncratic rather than smooth their prose, because polish now reads as machine-generated. The same dynamic applies to institutional communications, where a statement that skips acknowledging human impact can read as self-protective rather than considered.

There used to be a legible difference between a document someone built carefully and one built quickly. AI eliminated that difference faster than most communications functions noticed.

A recent essay by Nolen Royalty, published on eieio.games, named what’s missing “legibility of effort,” the capacity to recognize on sight whether a human put in real work.

Wired reported on July 29, 2026 that media institutions have arrived at the same realization. Pitchfork’s editors, led by head of editorial content Mano Sundaresan, sent writers a formal memo banning AI use in review copy and encouraging first-person voice. Aeon editor Richard Fisher described urging writers to be “more human,” rejecting the impulse to sand away roughness, because AI cannot write with the same idiosyncrasy. Novelists described deliberately avoiding em dashes and neat three-item lists, syntax now widely feared among writers to read as AI, to prove a human wrote the sentence.

I’ve made this call directly. An organization I advised wanted to announce internal process changes following a financial misstep without addressing the anger it had caused among its members. I advised the opposite: that the statement needed to acknowledge member anger plainly, because a response that skipped the human impact would read as management protecting itself rather than reckoning with what happened.

The consequence reaches past style, into whether readers can still assume human judgment produced an institution’s public statements, and whether they’re right to. Communications leaders who haven’t identified this problem are already managing its consequences, whether or not they’ve traced them back to the cause.
